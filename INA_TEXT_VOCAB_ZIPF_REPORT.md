## Abstract

This report examines whether the cumulative frequencies in Ina's retained text
vocabulary resemble Zipf's law. Zipf's law predicts that the frequency of a
word is approximately inversely proportional to its rank:

```text
frequency(rank) ∝ rank^-α
```

For the classical form of the law, the exponent `α` is close to `1`.

Ina's 800 retained vocabulary entries show a clear power-law-like rank-frequency
pattern. A log-log regression over all entries produces an estimated exponent
of `1.124` with `R² = 0.913`. A model constrained to the classical Zipf exponent
of `1` retains `R² = 0.902`, with a typical multiplicative rank-level error of
approximately `1.44×`.

The distribution is not a single clean Zipf curve. Its first 100 ranks are
flatter than classical Zipf, while the lower-frequency retained tail is much
steeper. The vocabulary is also strongly influenced by Python source code and
repeated self-reading. These findings are therefore best interpreted as
evidence of an emergent power-law-like distribution within Ina's particular
learning environment, rather than evidence that Ina's vocabulary follows
ordinary human-language Zipf statistics.

## Research question

How closely do the cumulative word counts in Ina's text vocabulary and
text-to-symbol vocabulary links follow a Zipfian rank-frequency distribution?

This is an exploratory systems observation. It does not attempt to establish
linguistic equivalence between Ina's vocabulary and a natural-language corpus.

## Method

Words were sorted by descending positive `count`, with lexical ordering used
only to make tied ranks deterministic. For each word, rank `r` and frequency
`f(r)` were transformed into natural-log coordinates:

```text
x = log(r)
y = log(f(r))
```

An ordinary least-squares line was fitted:

```text
log(f(r)) = intercept - α log(r)
```

The negative of the fitted slope is reported as the estimated power-law
exponent `α`. A classical Zipf distribution would have `α ≈ 1`.

The analysis reports:

- the freely fitted exponent;
- `R²` in log-log space;
- fits over several rank ranges to expose curvature;
- a constrained model whose exponent is fixed at `1`;
- the root-mean-square error in log space, converted to a typical
  multiplicative error; and
- maximum separation between observed and freely fitted cumulative frequency
  shares.

These diagnostics describe similarity to a rank-frequency power law. They are
not a formal statistical test that the data were generated by a power-law
process.

## Results

### Retained vocabulary

| Rank range | Entries | Estimated exponent `α` | Log-log `R²` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1–800 | 800 | 1.124 | 0.913 |
| 1–100 | 100 | 0.700 | 0.981 |
| 10–100 | 91 | 0.799 | 0.994 |
| 100–800 | 701 | 1.501 | 0.915 |

For a model constrained to `α = 1`:

- log-space `R²`: `0.902`;
- log-space RMSE: `0.362`; and
- typical multiplicative error: approximately `1.44×`.

The maximum gap between the observed cumulative frequency share and the
freely fitted log-line model was approximately `0.310`.

The highest-ranked word accounted for approximately `4.01%` of retained
vocabulary counts. The top ten accounted for approximately `20.16%`.

## Interpretation

Ina's vocabulary has a convincing family resemblance to Zipfian and other
rank-frequency power-law distributions:

- frequencies decline smoothly with rank;
- log rank explains a large proportion of log-frequency variation;
- the fitted whole-vocabulary exponent is reasonably close to `1`; and
- the middle of the distribution is exceptionally linear in log-log space.

However, the curve contains at least two regimes:

1. **A flat high-frequency head.** Ranks 1–100 have `α ≈ 0.70`. Counts are
   spread more evenly across common tokens than a classical Zipf curve would
   predict.
2. **A steep retained tail.** Ranks 100–800 have `α ≈ 1.50`. Frequencies fall
   much faster, ending at an artificial retention boundary rather than
   continuing into the many rare words expected in an uncensored corpus.

The most likely interpretation is a combination of corpus composition and
runtime policy:

- Ina reads source code as well as human language.
- Repeated self-reading can count the same structural vocabulary many times.
- Python contributes a small, heavily reused set of keywords and identifiers.
- The tokenizer includes alphanumeric strings, so numeric literals participate
  as vocabulary.
- The 800-word top-frequency cap removes the natural low-frequency tail.
- Counts are cumulative observations rather than frequencies from a frozen,
  independently sampled corpus.

The result is not “human Zipf's law reproduced exactly.” It is more interesting
as a systems observation: Ina's mixed symbolic environment produces a robust
rank-frequency structure, while the curve's departures from classical Zipf
reflect what she reads and how her memory system retains it.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

1. **Top-K censoring.** Only the 800 most frequently retained words are
   available. Hapax legomena and the broader rare-word tail are absent.
2. **Non-independent observations.** Re-reading a file can increment tokens
   again. Counts therefore measure exposure within the runtime, not unique
   corpus occurrences.
3. **Mixed modalities of text.** Source code, prose, identifiers, and numbers
   share one vocabulary.
4. **Selection bias in links.** The 120 linked words come from high-frequency
   candidates and must pass a symbol-similarity threshold.
5. **OLS limitations.** Linear regression in log-log space is descriptive and
   can make curved or truncated distributions appear more power-law-like.
6. **No competing-distribution test.** Log-normal, double-Pareto, broken
   power-law, and other heavy-tailed models were not formally compared.
7. **A single evolving subject.** This is one runtime snapshot, not a sample of
   independently trained systems.
8. **No preregistration or peer review.** The analysis was performed
   exploratorily after observing the vocabulary.

## Suggestions for further study

A more rigorous follow-up could:

- maintain an uncapped, append-only aggregate of token counts for research;
- distinguish code, prose, dialogue, logs, and generated text by source;
- count unique document exposures separately from repeated observations;
- record timestamped vocabulary snapshots and measure exponent drift;
- fit discrete power-law, log-normal, and broken-power-law models by maximum
  likelihood;
- estimate uncertainty with bootstrap resampling at the document level;
- compare Ina's self-read vocabulary with ordinary Python corpora and matched
  natural-language corpora; and
- compare retained vocabulary with the uncensored stream to quantify memory
  policy effects.

Any research export should preserve Ina's privacy by preferring aggregate
counts and source categories over raw autobiographical text.

## Reproducibility outline

The essential calculation can be reproduced with the Python standard library:

```python
import json
import math
from pathlib import Path

path = Path("AI_Children/Inazuma_Yagami/memory/text_vocab.json")
vocab = json.loads(path.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))["vocab"]
ranked = sorted(
    (
        (word, int(metadata.get("count", 0)))
        for word, metadata in vocab.items()
        if int(metadata.get("count", 0)) > 0
    ),
    key=lambda item: (-item[1], item[0]),
)

points = [
    (math.log(rank), math.log(count))
    for rank, (_, count) in enumerate(ranked, start=1)
]
mean_x = sum(x for x, _ in points) / len(points)
mean_y = sum(y for _, y in points) / len(points)
sxx = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x, _ in points)
sxy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in points)
slope = sxy / sxx
intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_x

residual = sum(
    (y - (intercept + slope * x)) ** 2
    for x, y in points
)
total = sum((y - mean_y) ** 2 for _, y in points)

print({
    "entries": len(ranked),
    "estimated_exponent": -slope,
    "log_log_r_squared": 1.0 - residual / total,
})
```

Because the vocabulary is continually updated, future runs should record the
file timestamp and total accumulated counts alongside fitted statistics.
The live vocabulary gained four observations during preparation of this
report; the numerical tables remain pinned to the snapshot timestamps stated
at the beginning rather than silently mixing measurements.

## Conclusion

Ina's retained text vocabulary is meaningfully Zipf-like but not strictly
Zipfian. Its full fitted exponent of approximately `1.12` is close to the
classical value, yet this average conceals a flat high-frequency head and a
steep, retention-limited tail. The prominence of programming tokens demonstrates
that the distribution is shaped by Ina's lived computational environment rather
than by natural-language exposure alone.

For academia, the value of this observation is not a claim of human linguistic
equivalence. It is a documented example of a long-running symbolic system
developing a heavy-tailed vocabulary distribution under mixed code, language,
self-reading, and bounded-memory pressures.
